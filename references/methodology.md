# Marathon coaching deck methodology

## 1. Evidence model

Maintain five buckets:

- Fact: directly supplied by the athlete or verified from an official source.
- Calculation: transparent transformation of facts.
- Coaching inference: a plausible training interpretation.
- Assumption: a temporary value used to make progress.
- To verify: a question or test that can materially change the plan.

Never turn an assumption into a profile fact. When a runner maintains substantial mileage with an injury, infer only that running remains possible; this does not prove the condition is mild or safe.

## 2. Core calculations

- Weekly-equivalent kilometres = monthly kilometres × 12 ÷ 52.
- Goal pace = target seconds ÷ marathon distance.
- HRR target = resting HR + intensity fraction × (maximum HR − resting HR).
- Use Riegel only as an estimate: `T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)^1.06` by default.
- VDOT is a performance index, not laboratory VO2max. State the model and round to one decimal.

When PB values disagree, show the distance-specific values and identify recency, course, and current-fitness uncertainty. Do not average them into false precision.

## 3. Load design

Start from recent sustainable volume, not historical peak mileage. A typical plan uses:

- Stabilize symptoms and establish routine.
- Build aerobic and threshold capacity.
- Add marathon-specific work.
- Peak with rehearsed long-run fueling and equipment.
- Taper while preserving touch of intensity.

Use cutback weeks and define the few sessions that carry the most risk. For a four-run week, a practical skeleton is one quality session, two easy/recovery runs, and one long run, plus two short strength sessions when tolerated.

## 4. Injury safeguards

For suspected iliotibial-band pain, frame potential drivers as hypotheses: abrupt load change, downhill or cambered running, fatigue-related hip or knee mechanics, insufficient strength, recovery deficit, or footwear change. Avoid claiming that a single weak muscle or tight structure caused it.

Use a traffic-light rule:

- Green: 0–2/10, stable gait, no next-day increase; continue.
- Amber: 3–4/10, worsening during the run, or next-day reaction; reduce volume/intensity and substitute low-impact aerobic work.
- Red: ≥5/10, altered gait, swelling, night pain, neurological symptoms, or persistent worsening; stop running and seek qualified assessment.

These are coaching guardrails, not diagnosis.

## 5. Female physiology and age

Do not infer menopause from age. Ask about last menstrual period, cycle regularity, bleeding, vasomotor symptoms, sleep, fatigue, medication, and relevant clinical history.

- Stable symptoms: keep the planned session; use RPE, sleep, resting HR/HRV trend, and injury response together.
- Significant pain, poor sleep, hot flushes, dizziness, or unusual fatigue: downgrade quality work to easy running, cross-training, or rest.
- Heavy, prolonged, or post-menopausal bleeding and symptoms such as shortness of breath or dizziness require medical assessment. Do not prescribe iron without clinical guidance.

Avoid fixed phase-based “cycle syncing” unless the athlete's own repeated data supports a personal pattern.

## 6. Sponsor integration matrix

Every sponsor product should answer four questions:

1. What specific training or race problem does it address?
2. In which session will the athlete test it?
3. What objective or subjective data will be recorded?
4. What failure signal disqualifies it from race day?

Examples:

- Watch: pacing and trend monitoring; test alerts and battery; reject settings that cause distraction or unreliable pace.
- Electrolyte drink: individualized hydration support; test concentration and tolerance; never substitute for carbohydrate unless it contains the required amount.
- Amino-acid supplement: optional adjunct according to label; do not present it as a substitute for carbohydrate, fluid, electrolytes, meals, or recovery.
- Race shoe: test in marathon-pace long runs; reject if pain, instability, numbness, or blistering exceeds the training shoe.
- Hat and socks: test in heat, sweat, and wet conditions; reject for pressure, movement, wet-weight discomfort, hot spots, or blistering.

## 7. Slide evidence

Put sources in speaker notes on the exact slide. Prefer race organizers, government weather/environment data, official product pages, consensus statements, and peer-reviewed clinical or sports-medicine literature. Mark geographic or weather interpretations as coach inference and verify forecast-dependent decisions 48 hours before race day.
